Classical Music

Introduction

Classical music generally refers to the art music of the Western world, distinct from folk or popular traditions. It is characterized by formality, complexity, and the use of polyphony. Since at least the ninth century, it has been primarily a written tradition, spawning a sophisticated notational system, as well as accompanying literature in analytical, critical, historiographical, musicological and philosophical practices.

Medieval Music (c. 500–1400)

Early medieval music was mostly religious, monophonic, and vocal. Western plainchant gradually unified into Gregorian chant. Musical centers existed at abbeys such as Saint Gall and Saint Martial, and composers began experimenting with polyphony. By the 11th century, staff notation was developed, allowing more complex compositions.

Renaissance Music (1400–1600)

The Renaissance period was characterized by greater use of instrumentation, multiple interweaving melodic lines, and the use of earlier forms of bass instruments. Social dancing became more widespread, so musical forms appropriate to accompanying dance began to standardize. It is in this time that the notation of music on a staff and other elements of musical notation began to take shape. Composers like Josquin des Prez and Palestrina developed intricate vocal and instrumental textures. Dance music and secular compositions flourished alongside sacred music, and musical notation became increasingly standardized.

Baroque Music (1580–1750)

The Baroque era saw the relative standardization of common-practice tonality, as well as the increasing importance of musical instruments, which grew into ensembles of considerable size. Opera, the concerto genre and the fugue became prominent. Composers like Johann Sebastian Bach, Antonio Vivaldi, and George Frideric Handel exemplified the era’s ornate style and formal structures.

Classical Period (1730–1820)

The Classical period emphasized clarity, balance, and structured forms. It established many of the norms of composition, presentation, and style, and was when the piano became the predominant keyboard instrument. The basic forces required for an orchestra became somewhat standardized (though they would grow as the potential of a wider array of instruments was developed). Composers such as Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart, Joseph Haydn, and Ludwig van Beethoven created widely admired representatives of absolute music, including symphonies, string quartets and concertos. Music focused on harmony, proportion, and emotional expression within formal frameworks.

Romantic Music (1800–1910)

The music of the Romantic era was characterized by increased attention to an extended melodic line, as well as expressive and emotional elements, paralleling romanticism in other art forms. Musical forms began to break from Classical era forms, with free-form pieces like nocturnes, fantasias, and preludes being written where accepted ideas about the exposition and development of themes were ignored or minimized. Composers like Chopin, Wagner, Mahler, and Tchaikovsky emphasized drama, nationalism, and imagination in symphonies, operas, and piano works. The music became more chromatic, dissonant, and tonally colorful.

20th and 21st Century Classical Music

At the turn of the century, music was characteristically late romantic in style with its expressive melodies, complex harmonies, and expansive forms. This era was marked by the works of several composers who pushed forward post-romantic symphonic writing. Composers such as Gustav Mahler and Richard Strauss continued to develop the western classical tradition with expansive symphonies and operas, while the likes of Jean Sibelius and Vaughan Williams infused their compositions with nationalistic elements and influences from folk songs. Today, classical music is performed worldwide and continues to evolve.
